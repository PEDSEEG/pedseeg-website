About the channel

Current standards for assessing depth of anesthesia rely on indirect measures — heart rate, blood pressure, muscle tone, and presumed drug interactions. Modern systems-level neuroscience now lets us link recognizable EEG signatures to the anesthetic states produced by most commonly used drugs. Familiarity with these signatures helps anesthesia clinicians make more detailed and accurate assessments of the anesthetic state of their patients. These videos provide a brief, accessible introduction to the EEG signal as it relates to altered states of arousal caused by anesthesia.

Interactive simulations

Three browser-based simulations and an EEG file viewer, all built by SmartTIVA, a PALNET collaborator. Unlike a video or a paper, these let you change the inputs and watch the signal respond — the learning is active rather than passive. Nothing to install, and they run on a laptop or a phone.

Start here

Sine wave combinator

Sum four sine waves in real time and change their frequencies to watch a composite trace separate into the rhythms underneath it. This is the idea every spectrogram rests on, and the quickest way to make it concrete.

Open the simulation →

Next

3D DSA rotation grid

Rotate a spectrogram in three dimensions to see how the familiar flat heat map is built — frequency, time and power stacked into a single surface. Useful for anyone reading the vertical DSA used in neuromonitoring.

When ready

Raw EEG & artifact sandbox

Signal and noise, grounded in Ohm's law, with the clinical scenarios that degrade a trace in theatre. Artifact is one of the hardest things to teach at the bedside — here you can generate it deliberately and see what it does.

Open the simulation →

Your own recordings

EDF viewer

Open an EEG recording saved as an EDF file and see two channels as a mirrored DSA, with the raw EEG for any 10-second stretch you click on. You can compare channels, adjust the color scale, zoom in on part of the recording and export the spectrogram as an image. SmartTIVA's viewer reads the file in your browser rather than uploading it; even so, use de-identified recordings.
